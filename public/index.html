Method details

Independent curves: Pₕ(z) = aₕ + bₕz + cₕz² and Pₗ(z) = aₗ + bₗz + cₗz², with z = (V − V₀) / S. Each branch has its own coefficients. V₀ and S only rescale volume for numerical stability.

Least squares: choose each branch’s coefficients to minimize Σ[Pᵢ − Pfit(Vᵢ)]² over its selected points. Points assigned to neither branch have no effect on the fits.

Intersection: solve (cₕ − cₗ)z² + (bₕ − bₗ)z + (aₕ − aₗ) = 0, then Vsat = V₀ + Sz and Psat = Pₕ(z). A crossing must lie near the selected branch ends and have decreasing fitted pressure. Ambiguous crossings are flagged.

Fit quality: RMSE = √[Σ(Pᵢ − Pfit)² / n], in pressure units. R² = 1 − Σ(Pᵢ − Pfit)² / Σ(Pᵢ − Pmean)². R² is undefined for a constant-pressure branch. Neither metric proves Psat accuracy.

Automatic suggestions: the current heuristic compares candidate outer regions using normalized pressure RMSE plus a penalty for skipped bend points. It can skip up to 8 points or 25% of nonzero-pressure readings, whichever is smaller. Always review the proposal; it is not a validated physical phase detector.

Limits: quadratic curves cannot represent every fluid or test path. Long extrapolations, nearly parallel trends, noisy readings, or unsuitable selections can move the intersection. Validate on reference tests before relying on the result.

Each branch is fitted independently as pressure versus volume. Quadratic least-squares curves follow the selected measurements without being forced through every noisy point. Dashed extensions find the crossing; neither branch is forced to meet a chosen point. The automatic suggestion may leave several bend points unassigned and is only a starting point for review.

This is a graphical Psat estimate, not a thermodynamic phase confirmation. Test temperature is optional for fitting; when unknown, the result has no reported temperature. Review phase identity using your test observations. No calibrated confidence percentage is claimed. Use for constant-temperature expansion segments with unchanged overall composition.

About this tool

A digital version of the flexible ruler used to estimate saturation pressure from your laboratory P–V graph.

1. Start with measured points

You provide pressure and volume readings from an equilibrated, constant-temperature test segment. The graph shows volume along the bottom and pressure up the side. Temperature can be left blank if it is unavailable.

2. Fit two separate trends

One trend follows each side of the change in behavior. Each has its own gently curved equation. Points around the bend can remain visible without belonging to either fit, just as on your paper graph.

3. Find where they cross

The tool extends the two trends toward one another. The pressure at their crossing is the graphical Psat estimate. The curves are not forced to meet a measured point.

4. Review before accepting

Check which points were used, how far each trend was extended, and the fit errors. A curve that follows points closely can still give an uncertain crossing. Dew points and near-critical samples can be especially difficult to resolve.

The equations, without the jargon

P = a + bz + cz² describes one gently curved trend. a shifts it up or down, b changes its slope, and c controls its curvature. Here z = (V − V₀) / S is rescaled volume, used to keep the calculations stable. Each branch has its own a, b and c.

We choose the coefficients that minimize the squared pressure differences between the selected points and the curve. Then we solve P_high(V) = P_low(V) to find the crossing. Points marked “Neither fit” contribute to neither equation.

RMSE tells you the typical pressure deviation from the selected points. R² describes how closely the trend follows their pressure variation. These are fit measures, not a percentage guarantee for Psat accuracy. Exact equations and coefficients appear with your results.

Bubble point and dew point

Bubble point: at the boundary, the first gas appears from a liquid when entering the gas–liquid region. Dew point: the first liquid appears from a gas when entering that region. At equilibrium, reversing the test path changes whether that phase appears or disappears; it does not automatically reverse the P–V axes.

In a typical CCE pressure reduction, both the bubble point and the upper (retrograde) dew point may be approached from higher pressure. A lower dew boundary has a different arrangement of phases, so select the boundary that matches your procedure. This tool does not calculate a phase envelope or confirm phases from P–V data alone.
